# How analogue clocks are taught to young children

Research for ticket #83. Question: what order and methods do schools and early-years sources use to teach reading an analogue clock, and what does that mean for a clock game mode where the player winds geared hands on a D-pad, the minute hand moves in five-minute steps, and early rungs may lock or hide a hand?

## Short answer

Every source agrees on the ladder: **o'clock, then half past, then quarter past and quarter to, then five minutes, then single minutes.** UK schools start with the **hour hand on its own**: it points straight at a number for o'clock and sits halfway between two numbers for half past. The minute hand comes in later, as a **second number line that counts in fives**. "Past" (minute hand on the right half) comes before "to" (left half), and children find "to" harder. Research says the hour hand is the hard one: children read it from the number it is *nearest* or *looks like it points at*, not from the gap it sits in. The well-known error is reading 2:50 as 3:50.

## 1. The order of the rungs

### UK National Curriculum (statutory, England)

Source: [National curriculum in England: mathematics programmes of study](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/national-curriculum-in-england-mathematics-programmes-of-study/national-curriculum-in-england-mathematics-programmes-of-study)

| Year (age) | Statutory | Non-statutory guidance |
|---|---|---|
| Year 1 (5-6) | "tell the time to the hour and half past the hour and draw the hands on a clock face to show these times" | "telling the time throughout the day, first using o'clock and then half past"; "connect turning clockwise with movement on a clock face" |
| Year 2 (6-7) | "tell and write the time to five minutes, including quarter past/to the hour"; "know the number of minutes in an hour" | "They connect the 5 multiplication table to the divisions on the clock face." |
| Year 3 (7-8) | "estimate and read time with increasing accuracy to the nearest minute"; Roman numerals I-XII; 12- and 24-hour | |

Before Year 1, there is no clock-reading requirement in early years; children just learn the language of time and daily routines.

### US Common Core (for comparison)

The order is the same, a little later: Grade 1 is hours and half hours (1.MD.B.3), Grade 2 is "to the nearest five minutes" ([2.MD.C.7](https://www.thecorestandards.org/Math/Content/2/MD/)), and Grade 3 is to the nearest minute (3.MD.A.1).

### Research on the order

- Friedman & Laycock (1989), *Children's analog and digital clock knowledge*, Child Development 60, 357-371, tested children aged about 6.5 to 10.5. Their main findings, as summarised by [Metelerkamp (2014), Rhodes University SANC teacher reading](https://www.ru.ac.za/media/rhodesuniversity/content/sanc/documents/Teacher_Reading_Material_on_Analogue_ClockTime.pdf):
  - "past the hour" times (11:20) are read more reliably than "to the hour" times (11:40), which they call a "right sector advantage";
  - 3, 6, 9 and 12 act as landmarks for the quarters.
  - (Only the secondary summary was checked; the original is paywalled.)
- Burny, Zhao, Valcke, Desoete et al. (2013), *Curriculum sequencing and the acquisition of clock reading skills among Chinese and Flemish children* ([record](https://search.fid-benelux.de/Record/base-29065873)). Chinese children taught the whole clock in one go at age six mastered it about two years earlier than Flemish children taught in stages over several grades. The authors argue that the Western age stages are "not universal, nor the most effective". So the ladder is a convention, not a law of development. (Abstract only.)
- Rothera (1989), *An examination of how children read the time*, Durham MA thesis ([Durham e-Theses](https://etheses.durham.ac.uk/id/eprint/6561)). In 120 children aged 6-9:
  - analogue reading scored lower than digital;
  - *setting* a clock scored lower than reading one.

  The second point matters because winding hands is a setting task.

## 2. One-handed clock: the hour hand first

- **NCETM Year 1, Unit 11 Time** ([NCETM](https://ncetm.org.uk/classroom-resources/cp-year-1-unit-11-time/)). The sequence is:
  1. a 0-12 number line, walked out loud ("o'clock in the morning");
  2. the line joined into a circle (a rope or hosepipe), with lap one as morning and lap two as afternoon;
  3. the hour hand alone: "When it points directly at a number, this is when it is o'clock";
  4. "only when this is secure", half past: "When the hour hand is halfway between two numbers it has gone past a number, so we say half past";
  5. practice "with the hour hand alone, first on o'clock times, then half past".

  Year 1 never brings in the minute hand.
- **Oak National Academy**, Year 1 lesson *Tell the time to the hour using the hour hand* ([Oak](https://www.thenational.academy/teachers/lessons/tell-the-time-to-the-hour-using-the-hour-hand)). Key point: "The short hand on a clock face shows which hour we are at or near." It "deliberately chooses to present clocks featuring just an hour hand to really focus in on its purpose". Two-handed o'clock comes "later in the unit".
- **Oak**, *Tell the time to the hour and half hour using the hour and minute hands* ([Oak](https://www.thenational.academy/teachers/programmes/maths-primary-ks1/units/time-sequencing-events-and-telling-the-time-to-the-hour-and-half-hour/lessons/tell-the-time-to-the-hour-and-half-hour-using-the-hour-and-minute-hands)). Key points:
  - "The long hand points to 12 when the time is on the hour."
  - "The minute hand moves all the way round the clock in one hour."
  - "When the minute hand is pointing to the 6, it is half past."
- **Ireland, Primary Mathematics Toolkit** (curriculumonline.ie), *Strategies and tools for teaching Time* ([PDF](https://www.curriculumonline.ie/getattachment/0196c2d2-3b03-43e3-a457-9f8f03ba0318/PMC_SM_Strategies-and-tools-for-teaching-Time.pdf)): "Using a one-handed clock (hour hand only) is a means of providing additional support for some children." It also advises saying "hour hand and minute hand rather than the big hand and the small hand."
- **Practitioner view** ([Graham Fletcher, 2016](https://gfletchy.com/2016/10/31/the-one-handed-clock-in-a-digital-era/)): children first "own the estimation of time using the hour hand", then the minute hand adds "precision". On the hour hand alone, children judge "at the number or half way in between".

The idea behind all of this: the hour hand on its own already tells the time roughly ("just after 3", "halfway between 3 and 4"). The minute hand is a more precise reading of the same thing, not a separate fact.

## 3. The minute hand counts in fives

- **NCETM Year 2, Unit 11 Time** ([NCETM](https://ncetm.org.uk/classroom-resources/cp-year-2-unit-11-time/)):
  - Check that the Year 1 hour circle is secure "before you introduce the minute hand".
  - Build a 0-60 minute number line, walk it, then bend it into a circle.
  - "Draw attention to the link to their knowledge of counting in fives": 1 is one five (5 minutes), and so on up to twelve fives (60).
  - Then lay the hour line and the minute line on one face, and read **the hour hand first, then the minute hand**.
  - "Only focus on minutes past the hour at this stage", then move on to "to" times.
- **National Curriculum Year 2:** "connect the 5 multiplication table to the divisions on the clock face" (link above).
- The clock is hard because it is not base ten: the numeral 1 means 5 minutes when the minute hand points at it and 1 o'clock when the hour hand does (Burny, Valcke & Desoete 2012, *Clock reading: an underestimated topic in children with mathematics difficulties*, J. Learning Disabilities 45, 351-360, as summarised by citing papers).
- Earnest, Gonzales & Plant (2018), *Time as a Measure: Elementary Students Positioning the Hands of an Analog Clock*, Journal of Numerical Cognition ([open access](https://jnc.psychopen.eu/index.php/jnc/article/view/5793)). Counting in fives from 12 ("unit iteration") placed the minute hand correctly 89.5% of the time, against 51.8% for matching to the numeral.

## 4. The "past" half and the "to" half

- **Oak**, *Tell and write the time to five minutes past and to* ([Oak](https://www.thenational.academy/teachers/programmes/maths-primary-ks1/units/time-write-and-tell-the-time-to-five-minutes/lessons/tell-and-write-the-time-to-five-minutes-past-and-to-on-a-clock-face)):
  - "Minutes past" is "up to 30 minutes past". "Minutes to" means the minutes left "to the next hour (after 30 minutes past)".
  - "We can use how many minutes 'past' to help us say how many minutes 'to'."
  - Misconception: "Pupils may overgeneralise, thinking, for example, when it points to 9, it is 9 minutes to." The 10 is "unusual", because there it means 10 minutes to.
- **Past is taught first, and it is easier:** NCETM Year 2 starts with "past" only. Friedman & Laycock's right-sector advantage says the same.
- **Language trap:** the hour named changes when the minute hand crosses 6. "Twenty to *four*" has the hour hand still between 3 and 4. Researchers call reading the coming hour once 30 minutes have passed the "passed hour rule" (Boulton-Lewis et al. 1997; Friedman & Laycock 1989, in the [Rhodes summary](https://www.ru.ac.za/media/rhodesuniversity/content/sanc/documents/Teacher_Reading_Material_on_Analogue_ClockTime.pdf)). Other languages differ: Dutch, German and Afrikaans "half twee" means 1:30.

## 5. Common misconceptions

1. **Mixing up the hands.** Oak: "Pupils will often mix the hour hand and the minute hand, labelling each as the other", and "Pupils may see the minute hand as the hour hand, treating wherever the minute hand is pointing to as o'clock."
2. **Reading the hour when the hour hand is between two numbers** (the big one).
   - Williams (2012), *Image schemas in clock-reading: latent errors and emerging expertise*, Journal of the Learning Sciences 21(2), 216-246 ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/10508406.2011.553259)). Children use a "proximity" reading (whichever number the hand is nearest). That gives the most common error: 2:50 read as **3:50**, because the hour hand is almost at the 3. Williams argues that the expert view is a "container": the hour is the *gap* the hand is in, and it counts as 2 until the hand reaches 3.
   - Earnest et al. (2018): "the hour hand is significantly more challenging to position accurately than the minute hand". This held in both Grade 2 and Grade 4.
     - Children placed it by "number matching" (for 2:50, "The hour hand is 2, so it's 2", pointing it straight at the 2).
     - Almost all hour-hand successes were on o'clock times.
     - Only 1 of 48 children reasoned about how far between the numbers the hour hand should sit.
3. **Thinking the hour hand stays still while the minute hand moves.** This is the setting version of error 2: the child puts the hour hand right on the number for any time in that hour. Earnest's number-matching placements are exactly this.
4. **Reading the numeral as the minutes** ("pointing at 4 means 4 minutes"). The superimposed scales cause it (Burny et al. 2012; Kamii & Long, cited in the Rhodes summary).
5. **Mixing up past and to** ("9 means 9 minutes to"; Oak, above).
6. **12 means many things:** start, end, 0, 60, noon and midnight (Williams 2004, as listed in the Rhodes summary). Children also find it hard to see that the hands move continuously, with no start and no stop (NCETM's reason for walking the circle twice: "time does not stop").

## 6. What the clock face should show

- **Numbers 1-12 on the face, hour hand clearly shorter.** Oak's keywords: the hour hand is "the shorter hand", the minute hand is "the longer hand". NCETM builds the face from a 0-12 number line bent into a circle.
- **Minute numbers (5, 10 ... 60) around the outside** for the five-minutes rung. NCETM lays the 0-60 minute line over the hour line on one face, and the curriculum links the 5 times table "to the divisions on the clock face". Teaching clocks print these as an outer ring.
- **Minute marks:** only needed at the single-minute rung (Year 3). For five-minute work the 12 numerals are the steps.
- **Colour-coded past and to halves:** a common teaching-clock feature (for example the EasyRead "Time Teacher", and the [US6406301 clock teaching aid patent](https://patents.google.com/patent/US6406301)). One half is printed "past" and the other "to". This is practitioner and commercial guidance; no controlled study of colour coding was found.
- **Geared clocks:** classroom geared clocks move the hour hand as the minute hand winds. They are recommended mainly so children see the hour hand travel between numbers. This comes from practitioner sources; the research (Earnest 2017, *Clock Work*, JRME 48(2)) shows the tool a child uses changes how they reason, but the full text was not checked.
- Ireland's guidance prefers the words "hour hand" and "minute hand" over "big" and "little" hand.

## What this means for the game

1. **Hour-hand-only rungs are standard practice, not a crutch.** NCETM Year 1 and Oak both start with the minute hand absent. A first rung that **hides the minute hand** and asks only "point the little hand at 3" (o'clock), then "halfway between 3 and 4" (half past), is the school order exactly.
2. **Gearing is the main teaching win, and it is aimed at the main misconception.** The common errors (2:50 read as 3:50, hour hand put straight on the number) all come from not seeing the hour hand creep. If winding the minute hand visibly drags the hour hand a twelfth of a gap per five-minute step, the game shows the very thing children get wrong. Target times **just before the hour** (2:45, 2:50, 2:55) are the real test of the "container" idea, so put them late, deliberately.
3. **Five-minute steps match the school model**, where the minute hand counts in fives and each numeral is one five. Showing 5, 10 ... 60 on an outer ring (or counting it aloud per step) is the NCETM bridge. With five-minute steps, single minute marks can be left off entirely.
4. **Locking the minute hand at 12 (or 6)** while the player sets the hour is the two-handed version of the Year 1 lessons: o'clock is "the long hand points to 12", half past is "the minute hand points to the 6". Order the rungs as o'clock, half past, quarter past, quarter to, then five-minute past, then five-minute to. Keep "to" after "past"; it is reliably harder.
5. **Setting is harder than reading** (Rothera 1989), so a winding game is the harder direction. For a pre-schooler, who is a year or more below Year 1 in this ladder, the hour-only and o'clock rungs probably cover most of the playtime. Give big tolerance on where the hour hand lands, since the research says even 7-9 year olds place it badly, and accept "in the right gap" rather than an exact angle until the late rungs.

## Gaps and caveats

- Friedman & Laycock (1989), Williams (2012), Burny et al. (2009, 2012, 2013) and Earnest (2017) were checked through abstracts or citing summaries only, not full text. Earnest, Gonzales & Plant (2018) was read in full.
- NCETM's Unit 11 Time pages note that the topic "is not in the DfE 2020 guidance or the NCETM Mastery PD Materials", so there are no NCETM slides. The sequence above is NCETM's own curriculum-prioritisation text.
- No peer-reviewed evidence was found for colour-coded halves, or for one-handed clocks beating two-handed ones; both are practice wisdom.
